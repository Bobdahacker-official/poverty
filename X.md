# KernelLand: The Country That Was Coded Into Existence

Once there was nothing but a developer, a blinking cursor, and a very unreasonable idea.
The developer typed `create_country('KernelLand')`, pressed Enter, and accidentally founded a nation.
Nobody had asked to exist. The citizens were particularly annoyed about the lack of a loading screen.
Mr. Kernel, the toughest boy at school, took this personally.

## Chapter 1: Patchwork Square and the Unscheduled Consequences
In Patchwork Square, Mayor Byte counted the flags that had appeared overnight, each stitched from an unfinished variable.
The streets of KernelLand smelled of rain, warm circuits, and the faint panic of code running in production.
Every morning the sun rose because the developer had put a timer in a loop and forgotten to add a stop condition.
At school, Mr. Kernel ruled the playground with a leather jacket and a backpack full of error messages.
He could win any staring contest by refusing to blink until the other person timed out.
The teachers called him a difficult student; he called the teachers unsupported hardware.
When the bell rang, the students scattered, but Mr. Kernel stayed to interrogate the bell about who compiled it.
He had discovered that KernelLand existed only because a developer had typed it into existence.
He found this deeply insulting: nobody had consulted him about the country's name, weather, or cafeteria menu.
Mayor Byte suggested writing a polite letter, but Mr. Kernel declared politeness a deprecated feature.
At the town hall, the citizens debated whether a coded country could have a real constitution.
The baker argued that bread was real as long as somebody could eat it; the accountant asked for a receipt.
Mr. Kernel marched through Patchwork Square, shouting that he would personally confront the developer.
The local newspaper printed his threat beneath a smaller headline about a missing semicolon.
He studied a map, only to find that the developer's house had been drawn outside the country's borders.
A bus driver offered him a ticket, but Mr. Kernel insisted on traveling by the most ridiculous means possible.
He arrived at the airport carrying no luggage, only an enormous amount of confidence.
The security officer asked for a boarding pass; Mr. Kernel replied, "I AM THE BOARDING PASS."
Instead of entering the plane, he climbed onto its roof and stood there like an angry weather vane.
He stamped one foot and screamed, "BRING ME TO THE DEVELOPER!"
The aircraft, apparently intimidated by his volume, rolled toward the runway while the pilot called for help.
The tower ordered everyone to stop; Mr. Kernel responded by yelling the order back in uppercase.
Through a bizarre sequence of coded coincidences, the plane's navigation display now showed the developer's house.
The passengers were safely evacuated in KernelLand's most confusing emergency drill.
The unmanned storybook aircraft floated over Patchwork Square as though the developer had forgotten to implement gravity.
Police officers were already waiting outside the developer's house, having read Mr. Kernel's very public threats.
Mr. Kernel jumped down, rang the doorbell, and announced that he had come to file a complaint with his teeth.
The developer opened the door and said, "I can explain," which was not the correct thing to say.
Mr. Kernel swallowed the developer whole in one absurd cartoon gulp, shoes and keyboard included.
Inspector Mutex shouted, "YOU ARE UNDER ARREST," while the developer's muffled voice requested a rollback.
The police handcuffed Mr. Kernel before he could claim that eating someone was merely garbage collection.
At the station, Mr. Kernel demanded a lawyer, a sandwich, and a better-written origin story.
The developer emerged unharmed after a dramatic sneeze, then apologized for creating a country without a planning permit.
Back in KernelLand, Mayor Byte insisted that the citizens deserved to choose what happened next.
They voted to keep their strange country, rewrite its rules, and prohibit the use of aircraft as personal taxis.
Mr. Kernel listened from his cell and realized that being tough at school did not make him right.
He wrote an apology in careful lowercase letters, which everyone agreed was his most shocking act.
And in Patchwork Square, the country continued, not because the developer commanded it, but because its people chose to stay.
